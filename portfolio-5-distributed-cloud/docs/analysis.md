## 3. Replication (2.1.4.2) and consistency (2.1.4.3)
- **Asynchronous replication works and is quick on one machine:** after a write on the primary, the replica showed it after a median of `<ms>`, worst case `<ms>` in 30 tries (R2). Real lag depends on distance and load.
- **Eventual consistency, shown:** with replication delay injected on purpose (3 s, to emulate a slow link), a read from the replica right after a write returned the old stock value (`<old>`), the primary returned the new one (`<new>`), and 3.5 s later the replica agreed (R3). The delay is artificial, but the behaviour is exactly what a customer reading from a lagging replica would see. For Konga this means reads that must see a customer's own change should go to the primary.
- **Synchronous replication costs latency:** waiting for the replica changed single-row commit latency from `<ms>` (async) to `<ms>` and `<ms>` (R4). Over a real link each commit would also pay the round trip.

## 4. CAP and the trade-off (2.2.1)
- With synchronous replication and the replica unreachable, the primary **stopped answering writes** (client gave up after 4 s, R5). That is a CP choice: consistency kept, availability for writes lost.
- With asynchronous replication, the primary stayed available. When it then crashed before the replica received the last commit, the promoted replica **did not have that acknowledged write** (stock `<old>` instead of 555, R6). That is the AP-leaning choice: available, but not always consistent after failover (a recovery point larger than zero).
- Konga decision: stock and payments should use synchronous replication to at least one replica (or a quorum scheme) and accept slower commits; catalogue reads and reports use asynchronous replicas. This is a design argument supported by the measurements above, not something I deployed.

## 5. ACID versus BASE for Konga (2.2.2)
ACID tables: orders, stock, payments, payouts (Portfolio 3). BASE-style data: sessions, carts, counters, recommendations, shipment events (Portfolio 4). The split follows the cost of being briefly wrong: a stale view count harms nobody, a stale stock count sells an item twice.

## 6. What I did not test
No real network partition (I stopped the replica instead), no automatic failover manager (the promote was done by hand), no old-primary rebuild after failover, no failure between two shard commits (`postgres_fdw` does not use two-phase commit), and no multi-node NoSQL cluster.
